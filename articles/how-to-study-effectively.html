How to Study Effectively: A Complete Guide

Last updated: 19 November 2026

Many learners study for hours and still feel unprepared. The problem is often not effort but method. Some ways of studying feel productive without helping you remember much, while others feel harder but work far better. This guide explains what to do, step by step.

1. Know what you need to study

Start by listing the topics for your test or exam. Use your teacher’s topic list, your textbook’s contents page or your class notes. Mark each topic as “I know this”, “I half know this” or “I don’t know this”. Begin with the last two groups. Studying only what you already know feels comfortable but wastes time.

2. Study in short, focused blocks

Most people concentrate best in blocks of about 25 to 45 minutes, followed by a short break of five to ten minutes. Put your phone in another room during the block. Decide before you start what you will finish, for example “learn the three types of map scale and do five questions”.

3. Choose active methods, not just reading

Rereading notes and highlighting feel easy, but they do not test whether you can actually remember or use the information. Studies of learning techniques have repeatedly found that practising retrieval, which means testing yourself, and spreading your study over time work much better. Try these instead:

  • Close your notes and write down everything you remember about a topic, then check what you missed.
  • Turn headings into questions and answer them from memory.
  • Do practice questions or a worksheet, then mark it with the memo.
  • Explain the topic out loud as if you were teaching a friend.

Read more in our guides to active recall and spaced repetition.

4. Spread your study over days, not hours

Studying a topic for 30 minutes on four different days beats two hours the night before. Each time you return to a topic, you have to pull the information back from memory, and that makes it stick. Start revising at least two weeks before a big test if you can.

5. Mix topics when you practise

In subjects like Mathematics, practise a mix of question types instead of doing twenty questions of the same kind in a row. Mixing forces you to decide which method to use, which is exactly what a test asks you to do.

6. Make your notes short and useful

Good notes are shorter than the textbook. Write key ideas, definitions and an example for each, and use a mind map or a table when a topic has many parts. Then use the notes to test yourself instead of just reading them. See our free notes and mind maps for examples.

7. Look after your body

Sleep, food, water and some movement all affect how well you learn. Staying up very late to study usually costs more than it gains, because tired brains remember and think less well.

8. Check and adjust

After each test, look at which questions you lost marks on and why. Did you not know the content, run out of time, or misread the question? Change your study plan to fix the real problem.

A simple study session you can copy

  1. Choose one topic and set a 30-minute timer.
  2. Spend five minutes reading your notes.
  3. Close the notes and write down everything you remember for ten minutes.
  4. Check against your notes and mark what you missed.
  5. Spend ten minutes on practice questions about the parts you missed.
  6. Take a short break, and plan to revisit the topic in two or three days.

Common mistakes

  • Only rereading or highlighting.
  • Waiting until the night before.
  • Studying with notifications on.
  • Avoiding the topics you find hardest.
  • Copying out notes word for word without thinking about them.
