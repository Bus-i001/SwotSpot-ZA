How to find a good tutor in South Africa

Last updated: 19 November 2026

A good tutor can build confidence as well as marks, but a poor match wastes money and time. This guide helps learners and parents choose carefully.

1. Decide what you need help with

Before you search, write down the subject, the grade and the problem. Is it one difficult topic, general catching up, or exam preparation? A learner who struggles with fractions needs something different from one who wants to move from 60% to 80%. Check the learner's recent tests and ask the school teacher where the gaps are.

2. Where to look

  • Your school: ask the subject teacher or the school office, since many schools know trusted tutors or run extra lessons.
  • University students: students studying the subject can be affordable and relatable, especially for Grade 8 and 9.
  • Tutoring centres: these offer structure and several subjects in one place.
  • Online tutors: useful if you live far from options or want flexible times.
  • Word of mouth: parents and friends who have used a tutor can give honest feedback.

3. What makes a tutor a good fit

  • They know the curriculum your learner follows, such as CAPS or IEB.
  • They can explain things in more than one way and are patient with questions.
  • They give the learner practice and feedback, not just answers.
  • They communicate clearly with the parent about progress.
  • The learner feels comfortable with them.

4. Questions to ask before you start

  • What is your experience with this subject and grade?
  • Which curriculum do you teach?
  • How do you plan lessons and track progress?
  • Can we have a short trial lesson?
  • What are your fees, cancellation rules and lesson length?
  • Can I speak to a parent you have worked with?

5. Warning signs

  • They promise guaranteed marks or a quick fix.
  • They refuse a trial lesson or are unclear about fees.
  • They do the homework for the learner.
  • They cannot say what they will teach or how they will measure progress.
  • The learner dreads the lessons and the problem is not getting better.

6. Keep the learner safe

Safety comes first. For younger learners, have a parent or adult at home during lessons, or choose a public, supervised place. For online lessons, use a shared room rather than a bedroom and stay nearby. Keep communication on the parent's phone or email where possible, and trust your instincts if something feels wrong.

7. Check that it is working

After four to six lessons, look for real change: better test marks, more confidence, or homework getting done with less stress. Ask the tutor for a short progress update. If nothing improves, talk to the tutor about changing the plan, or try someone else.

8. Support the tutoring at home

Tutoring works best when learners also revise between lessons. Use our free notes, mind maps and worksheets for extra practice, and mark the worksheets with the memos together.

Quick checklist

  1. Know the subject and the gap.
  2. Ask for recommendations.
  3. Ask the key questions.
  4. Do a trial lesson.
  5. Check safety arrangements.
  6. Review progress after a few weeks.
