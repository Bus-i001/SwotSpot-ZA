Active Recall Explained

Last updated: 19 November 2026

Active recall means trying to remember information without looking at it. Instead of reading your notes again, you close them and ask yourself what you know. It feels harder than rereading, and that effort is exactly why it works.

Why it works

Every time you pull a fact out of your memory, you make it easier to find the next time. Researchers call this the testing effect, or retrieval practice. Rereading makes information feel familiar, which can trick you into thinking you know it. Active recall shows you what you really know, so you can spend your time on the gaps.

How to do it

  1. Study the topic first. Read your notes or watch the lesson once.
  2. Close everything. Put your notes and textbook away.
  3. Recall. Write down or say out loud everything you can remember, or answer questions about the topic.
  4. Check. Compare with your notes and mark what you missed or got wrong.
  5. Repeat the gaps. Study the missed parts, then test yourself on them again.

Ways to practise active recall

  • Blank-page recall: write everything you remember about a topic on an empty page.
  • Flashcards: put a question on one side and the answer on the other.
  • Question turning: change each heading in your notes into a question and answer it from memory.
  • Worksheets and past questions: try them without looking at your notes, then mark them with the memo.
  • Teaching: explain the topic aloud to a friend, a family member or an imaginary class.

An example

Say you are studying map skills. Instead of rereading your notes, close them and answer: “What are the three types of map scale?” and “How do I read the grid reference C4?” Write your answers, then check. If you forgot that a ratio scale such as 1 : 50 000 means 1 cm on the map is 50 000 cm on the ground, you now know exactly what to study again. You can try this with our Grade 8 Map Skills topic.

A 10-minute routine

  1. Pick one topic.
  2. Write five questions about it, using your notes or textbook.
  3. Close your notes and answer all five from memory.
  4. Check your answers and put a tick or a cross next to each.
  5. Keep the crossed questions for your next session.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Peeking too soon. Give yourself a real try before you look at the answer, even if you are not sure.
  • Only testing the easy parts. Make sure your questions cover the topics you find hard.
  • Doing it once. Come back to the same questions a few days later. This works even better together with spaced repetition.
  • Getting discouraged. Forgetting things during practice is normal. It tells you what to study next.
